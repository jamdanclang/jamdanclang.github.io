Nebraska Legislature 109th Legislature, 2nd Session

The Department of Water, Energy, and Environment (DWEE) and the Governor’s office prioritized the Perkins County Canal Project to secure Nebraska's legal entitlement under the South Platte River Compact [1-3]. LB 759 (passed April 2026) granted DWEE specific authority to enter private land for project surveys and geotechnical work [3-5]. Efforts to redirect $500M from this project to the Water Sustainability Fund via LB 673 and LB 674 failed to pass [6-8].

Natural Resources Districts (NRDs) manage local water rights through integrated management plans aimed at reducing consumptive use [9-11]. LB 807 (passed March 2026) shifted riparian vegetation management to DWEE to improve streamflow conveyance [12, 13].

Tribal Nations gained the right to acquire municipal waterworks located on tribal lands via LB 1250 [14, 15]. Additionally, transparency regarding water rights surfaced in LB 1261, where amendments required industrial power providers to disclose projected water usage and potential impacts on agricultural and residential users [16].

Nebraska Legislature 109th Legislature, 1st Session

In the 109th Session, the executive branch consolidated authority over Nebraska's water. LB317 (Final Reading) merges the Department of Natural Resources into the new Department of Water, Energy, and Environment effective July 1, 2025 [1, 2]. This act creates the Chief Water Officer (CWO), a position appointed by the Governor with exclusive jurisdiction over water rights for irrigation, power, and other useful purposes [3-6].

Local Natural Resources Districts (NRDs) remain the "preferred entities" for regulating groundwater to prevent conflicts and contamination, working under CWO oversight to develop integrated management plans [7-10]. LB36 (passed) increases the CWO and NRDs' power over major users, requiring commercial or industrial entities using over 25 million gallons annually to provide mitigation reports starting January 1, 2026 [11, 12].

Proposed shifts in funding priorities were seen in LB673 and LB674, which sought to transfer up to $500 million from the Perkins County Canal Project to the Water Sustainability Fund; these proposals remained in committee [13-16].

Nebraska Legislature 108th Legislature, 2nd Session

During the 108th Legislature, Senator Jane Raybould (District 28) and the Natural Resources Committee have shown the most significant documented interest in water infrastructure and rights. On March 2, 2024, Raybould introduced LR352, a resolution for an interim study to assess $2.81 billion in identified needs for Nebraska’s clean and drinking water systems [1, 2]. The proposal emphasizes that the state currently funds only 15% of these projects, leaving a massive gap for contamination mitigation and infrastructure maintenance [2].

The Natural Resources Committee, chaired by Senator Bruce Bostelman, is designated to conduct this study [2, 3]. Agricultural interests also remain central; LR469 notes rising energy demands in the agriculture sector, and historical recognition was given to Ralph and Beverly Holzfaster for advancing pivot irrigation technology [3, 4]. Additionally, Senator DeKay highlighted the commercial significance of Nebraska's water-dependent agricultural products in LR456 [5, 6]. While LR352 identifies critical funding shortages, it remains a study proposal rather than a completed legislative outcome [2].

Nebraska Legislature 107th Legislature, 2nd Session

In the 107th Legislature’s 2nd Session, the Department of Natural Resources (DNR) and Governor Ricketts were primary advocates for Nebraska's water rights through LB1015 (Perkins County Canal Project Act), advanced in 2022 to secure South Platte River flows under the 1923 South Platte River Compact [1-3]. Supporting this effort were the Nebraska Farm Bureau, Nebraska Cattlemen, and various Natural Resources Districts (NRDs) [4].

Speaker Hilgers spearheaded LB1023 (JEDI Act), advanced in March 2022, to construct a 3,600-acre reservoir for flood control and tourism [5-7]. Senator Moser’s LB809 passed to modernize the Drinking Water State Revolving Fund Act [8-10]. Conversely, the Sierra Club and Bold Alliance expressed concern or opposition regarding the environmental impacts of these large-scale water projects [3, 11, 12].

Independent proposals that failed to pass include LB190 (Water Sustainability Fund restrictions) and LB191 (irrigation district elector qualifications), both introduced by Senator Hughes and indefinitely postponed, though components were amended into other bills [13, 14]. Senator Bostelman's LB806 successfully advanced to regulate powered drainage systems and prevent property harm [15, 16].

Nebraska Legislature 107th Legislature, 1st Session

During the 2021 session, water rights and protection were championed by the Natural Resources Committee, led by Senator Bostelman, who initiated studies on surface water irrigation infrastructure (LR117) and emergency responses to pesticide-related water threats (LR152) [1-3]. Senator Groene introduced LR192 to establish statutory definitions for water augmentation to ensure compact compliance and aquifer recharge [4]. Senator Blood focused on protection through LR159, investigating the AltEn plant’s potential contamination of the water table and Lincoln’s drinking supply [5, 6].

Key legislative outcomes included LB26 (Wayne), which provided a sales tax exemption for residential water service and was approved on May 25, 2021 [7-9]. LB384 and LB380 secured significant funding, including an $11 million transfer to the Water Sustainability Fund and appropriations for water quality and decommissioning [10-12]. Additionally, Natural Resources Districts (NRDs) maintained central roles in managing ground water quantity and quality [13, 14]. LR130 (Hunt) highlighted environmental concerns by supporting the revocation of the Keystone XL pipeline permit to protect water quality [15, 16].

Nebraska Legislature 106th Legislature, 2nd Session

During this session, Natural Resources Districts (NRDs), the Department of Natural Resources (DNR), and agricultural advocacy groups like the Nebraska Farm Bureau and Nebraska Cattlemen emerged as the primary stakeholders in water rights [1, 2].

Legislative efforts focused on defining ownership and management authority. Significant failed proposals included LB 802 (Jan 2020), which sought to declare groundwater rights inseparable from land ownership, but was indefinitely postponed [2, 3]. Similarly, LB 368 proposed eliminating "overappropriated" basin designations, and LB 845 (Groene) attempted to allow augmentation projects to retain pumping rights after selling real property; both were indefinitely postponed in early 2020 [4-7].

Successful legislation included LB 632, approved Aug 17, 2020, which modernized Natural Resources Commission membership while explicitly stating it lacks jurisdiction over water rights [8-10]. It also mandated a state flood mitigation plan [11]. Furthermore, LR 288 urged federal prioritization of flood control over wildlife protection in the Missouri River basin [12, 13].

Nebraska Legislature 106th Legislature, 1st Session

The Department of Natural Resources (DNR) and Natural Resources Districts (NRDs) are the primary entities managing Nebraska’s water rights [1-3]. Key legislative outcomes included LB48, which passed to allow 30-year "sufficient cause" nonuse extensions for water rights under crop reserve programs [4, 5]. LB177 (approved May 8, 2019) extended NRD authority to issue bonds for water projects through 2024 [6, 7]. LB319 reaffirmed DNR jurisdiction over irrigation and power water rights [2, 8].

Funding remained a priority; LB298 directed $11 million to the Water Sustainability Fund and $3.3 million to the Water Resources Cash Fund annually for FY2019-2021 [9, 10]. Significant proposals that did not pass included LB368, which sought to reclassify "overappropriated" basins as "fully appropriated" to alter NRD mandates [11, 12], and LB606, intended to let NRDs retain water rights for augmentation projects regardless of land ownership [13, 14]. Additionally, LB632 (Final Reading 2020) mandated a state flood mitigation plan following the historic 2019 floods [15-17].

Nebraska Legislature 105th Legislature, 2nd Session

During the 105th Legislature, Natural Resources Districts (NRDs), the Department of Natural Resources (DNR), and agricultural advocacy groups like the Nebraska Farm Bureau demonstrated the highest engagement with water rights [1-3]. NRDs exercise authority via tax levies for groundwater management and operate augmentation projects to meet interstate compact obligations [4-8].

A central outcome was LB758 (Hughes), approved February 28, 2018, which requires NRDs acquiring private land for streamflow augmentation to collaborate with counties and allows voluntary payments in lieu of taxes to protect local tax bases [9-11]. Conversely, several property-rights-focused proposals failed to pass. LB1123 (Groene), which sought to allow NRDs to retain groundwater rights while selling surface interests, was indefinitely postponed [12, 13]. LB1022 (Schumacher), the Irrigation Tax Act, proposed recognizing groundwater as a state asset and taxing its use for irrigation [14, 15]. Funding remained a priority, with LB945 authorizing a $10.67 million transfer to the Water Sustainability Fund in 2018 [16-18].

Nebraska Legislature 105th Legislature, 1st Session

In the 105th Legislature, water rights are primarily the concern of Natural Resources Districts (NRDs), the Department of Natural Resources (DNR), and agricultural landowners. LB98 (Friesen) successfully extended NRDs' three-cent tax levy authority until FY2025-26 to fund groundwater and integrated management activities [1-3].

Proposals reflecting intense scrutiny of interlocal augmentation projects like N-CORPE included LB218 (Groene), which sought to mandate public hearings and resolutions before public entities could pump groundwater for interstate compact compliance [4, 5]. However, LB488 (Groene), the Water Conservation Grant Act, which proposed using the Water Sustainability Fund to pay landowners to retire irrigated acres, was indefinitely postponed on February 15, 2017 [6, 7].

Agricultural interests pushed for valuation reforms; LB602 and related amendments (e.g., AM1353) proposed that property assessments for irrigated cropland account for well capacity and water availability affected by regulatory actions [8-10]. Additionally, LB182 (Hughes) passed, clarifying DNR/DEQ authority to provide safe drinking water loans to small municipalities [11, 12].
